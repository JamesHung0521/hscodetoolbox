Cost Breakdown of a Real FCL Shipment: From Factory to Destination

Practical guidance from HS Code Toolbox

This is a realistic 40ft full-container-load shipment of furniture from Guangzhou to Los Angeles. The exact numbers move around with the season and the lane, but the structure of the costs, and where the surprises hide, stays much the same. I've rounded the figures so it's easy to follow where the money actually goes.

At the factory, before the port

People always forget the origin side. Cartons and palletising materials, about $450. Trucking from factory to the warehouse or port, another $310. Customs clearance through the agent, around $120. Forwarder handling and origin charges, $180.

That's over a thousand bucks — usually $1,000 to $1,200 — and the container hasn't even seen the ocean yet. New exporters routinely underestimate this whole stage.

Ocean freight: the number everyone quotes

This is the headline figure, and for a lot of people it's the only one they remember. A 40ft to the US West Coast these days lands somewhere in the $3,800–$4,500 range, plus a couple hundred in surcharges.

It's the biggest single line, but it's nowhere near the whole story. It's also the part that swings the most — book at the wrong time and it can cost you thousands in difference.

Destination charges: where beginners get hurt

Most freight quotes quietly stop at the water's edge. What happens on arrival almost never makes it into the quote.

Terminal handling at the US end, around $350. Customs clearance through a broker, $180. The customs bond, $50. Then port or demurrage charges — anything from zero up to $400 if you're slow picking it up. Trucking to the final warehouse, $450. Telex release and document fees, $120.

Put together, those destination charges typically add up to $1,200 to $1,800 on top of the freight. If you priced your buyer at "freight only," that whole gap is coming out of your margin.

So what's the real total?

Origin and factory costs, ~$1,150. Ocean freight, ~$4,300. Destination, ~$1,450.

That's roughly $6,900 all in — and the ship itself is barely 60% of it. A quote that only looks at the freight is comparing less than two-thirds of the true cost.

Three things exporters keep missing

First, always ask for an all-in quote. A "sea freight rate" without destination charges, THC and doc fees isn't the real price.

Second, watch where the container sits. A half-empty FCL costs exactly the same as a full one, so you want to confirm you're actually filling the box.

Third, speed matters at both ends. Demurrage and detention start ticking the moment the container comes off the vessel — plan the pickup before the ship even arrives.

Every shipment will differ on the numbers, but that's fine. Treat a quote as incomplete until the destination charges are in writing. Once they are, the figure you compare across forwarders is finally the real one.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the ocean freight only part of the total shipping cost?

Because there are costs on both ends of the journey. At origin you have trucking, customs clearance, handling and documentation — typically around $1,000-$1,200. At destination there's terminal handling, customs brokerage, bonds, trucking and doc fees — typically $1,200-$1,800. The ocean freight itself is usually 55-65% of the total. Always ask for an all-in quote.

What are BAF and THC, and why do they appear as separate charges?

BAF (Bunker Adjustment Factor) is a fuel surcharge that carriers add to adjust for fluctuations in bunker fuel prices. THC (Terminal Handling Charge) covers the cost of moving the container within the port — from truck to stack to vessel, or vice versa. Both are standard line items that every shipment incurs. They're separated from the base freight rate because they vary independently.

How can I avoid demurrage and detention charges?

Plan your pickup before the ship arrives. Demurrage starts when the container sits in the port beyond the free days (usually 4-5 working days). Detention is charged when you keep the carrier's container out too long after pickup. Get your trucking arranged early, have customs clearance pre-filed, and confirm your warehouse is ready to receive. Every day of delay is real money — typically $100-$300 per day per container.

Is it normal for destination charges to equal or exceed the ocean freight?

On shorter routes, yes. On longer or more expensive lanes, the ocean freight dominates. But destination charges almost always surprise first-time importers because freight quotes often omit them. The honest approach: always budget $1,200-$1,800 for destination costs on a 40ft shipment to the US, on top of whatever the ocean freight quote says.

What's the difference between a freight quote and an all-in quote?

A freight quote only covers the ocean portion — port to port. An all-in quote includes origin charges (trucking, customs, handling), ocean freight with surcharges, and destination charges (THC, customs clearance, trucking, documentation). If a forwarder gives you only a freight rate, you're seeing maybe 60% of the real cost. Always request all-in before comparing forwarders.

How much does customs clearance typically cost at both ends?

At origin, customs clearance through an agent typically runs around $100-$150. At destination, a licensed customs broker charges roughly $150-$250 for a standard entry, plus the customs bond (around $50 for a single-entry bond). These are relatively fixed costs regardless of cargo value, so they matter proportionally more on smaller shipments.
