20ft vs 40ft vs 40HC: Which Container Fits Your Cargo?

Practical guidance from HS Code Toolbox

Picking the right container is a balance of volume, weight and cost. Here is how the three most common dry containers compare, and how to choose.

The three standard dry containers

  • 20ft (20'): roughly 33 CBM of usable space with a payload around 28,000 kg. The compact, heavy-duty option for dense or heavy goods.
  • 40ft (40'): about 67 CBM with a payload around 26,500 kg. The standard for most full-load shipments.
  • 40ft high-cube (40'HC): about 76 CBM with a similar payload to the 40ft. Around 30 cm taller, which matters for bulky, stackable cargo.

Think in volume AND weight

A 20ft and a 40ft are close in payload, but the 40ft carries twice the volume. So the rule of thumb:

  • Heavy, dense goods (steel, machinery, liquids) hit the weight limit long before the box is full — a 20ft often makes more sense and costs less.
  • Light, bulky goods (furniture, cartons of foam or textiles) fill the volume first — a 40ft or a 40HC uses the extra height well.

This is exactly the "bottleneck" your loading calculator should name: are you out of space first, or over weight first?

When the high-cube shines

Choose the 40HC when your cargo is volume-hungry but light — for example tall, stackable cartons. The extra height converts into real usable space. If your goods are near a 40ft weight limit, the high-cube adds nothing.

Compare before you book

Never book on price alone. Compute your total CBM and total weight, then check both against each candidate container. The cheapest-looking option is the one that wastes the least of whichever you will hit first.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if I need a reefer container?

If your product requires a specific temperature range during transit — typically perishables like fruit, meat, dairy, pharmaceuticals, or certain chemicals — you need a reefer. The key question is: does your product degrade, melt, or become unsafe outside a controlled temperature? If yes, reefer. They cost more and need to be plugged in at the port and on the vessel, so book early — reefer capacity is limited.

When should I use an open-top container instead of a standard one?

When your cargo is too tall to fit through the doors of a standard container — things like machinery, large pipes, or items that need crane loading from above. Open-tops have a removable tarpaulin roof instead of a steel roof. They're more expensive and less weatherproof, so only use one when your cargo literally won't fit in a standard box otherwise.

Is a flat rack container safe for heavy machinery?

Yes — flat racks are designed for exactly that. They have no side walls and no roof, just a reinforced floor with lashing points. They handle oversized, heavy, or oddly shaped cargo that can't fit inside a box. The trade-off is exposure to weather and sea spray, so your cargo needs to be weather-resistant or well-wrapped. Also, flat rack bookings are subject to space availability on deck.

What is the difference between a 40ft standard and a 40ft high-cube?

Same length and width, but the high-cube is about 30 cm taller inside (2.69m vs 2.39m). That extra height gives you roughly 12% more volume — 76 CBM vs 67 CBM. For bulky, lightweight goods like furniture, textiles, or empty plastic bottles, that extra space can mean the difference between needing two containers or fitting everything in one. The freight rate is usually similar.

Can I ship hazardous goods in any container type?

No. Hazardous goods (DG cargo) require specific container types and markings depending on the hazard class. Some DG can go in standard dry containers with proper labeling; others need ventilated containers, tank containers, or specially approved equipment. Always declare the correct IMDG class to your carrier upfront — shipping DG without declaration is illegal and dangerous.

How do I choose between a 20ft and a 40ft container?

It comes down to your cargo's weight and volume. Heavy, dense goods like steel, tiles, or liquids tend to max out weight before filling the space — a 20ft is usually enough and costs less. Light, bulky goods like furniture or textiles fill the volume first — go with a 40ft or 40HC to use the space efficiently. Calculate your total CBM and weight first, then match to the container that wastes the least of both.
