HS Code 101

What Is an HS Code and Why It Matters

Practical guidance from HS Code Toolbox

The HS code is the universal product identifier of international trade. Nearly every customs declaration, invoice and shipment references one — and choosing it correctly affects your duty rate, your compliance risk and sometimes whether your goods can be shipped at all.

The 6-digit foundation

The Harmonized System (HS) is a standardised list of about 5,000 product descriptions maintained by the World Customs Organization (WCO). Every product in global trade maps to a six-digit code:

  • First 2 digits — the chapter (e.g. 09 = coffee, tea, spices; 64 = footwear).
  • Next 2 digits — the heading within that chapter.
  • Last 2 digits — the subheading that narrows it down further.

So 6403 = chapter 64 (footwear) → heading 03 (footwear with outer soles of rubber, plastics or leather) → a specific subheading for leather-soled footwear. The first six digits are identical in every country that uses the HS.

Why the exact code matters

A few digits can change everything:

  • Duty rates differ. One subheading may be duty-free while a neighbour carries a high rate.
  • Licences and restrictions. Some codes require import/export permits or face bans.
  • Customs inspection. A wrong code is the most common reason shipments get held at the border.
  • Anti-dumping and quotas. Certain products fall under special measures tied to their code.

Getting it right isn't just about paperwork — it protects your money and your delivery time.

Different countries add more digits

Once goods reach a specific border, the 6-digit code is extended for national tariff and statistics. The USA uses 10-digit HTSUS numbers, the EU uses TARIC, and China uses its own 10-digit schedule. Two digits of six are global; the extra digits are local. That is why the same product can appear on customs forms with a different, longer code depending on destination.

Where to start

Start from what your product is, not what you hope it will be classified as. Search by name first, then read the legal notes in your tariff schedule before committing to a code. A short, careful lookup at the start saves a customs hold at the port.
