What Happens When You Overweight a Container?

Practical guidance from HS Code Toolbox

Overloading a container isn't just a paperwork slip. Every container has a maximum payload, and treating it as a suggestion is how shipments get stuck — or worse, become unsafe.

The port will catch it

Containers get weighed before they're loaded on the ship (the verified gross mass rule). If your box is over its rating, it won't board. The line refuses it, the ship sails without it, and you're left with a full shipment that's going nowhere on schedule.

It costs real money

A rejected container means rerouting, re-booking, storage and the line's overweight fees — sometimes the difference between a clean profit and a loss on that order. A few hundred kilos of overreach can run into thousands in cascading charges.

It's a genuine safety issue

The payload limit isn't conservative for fun. Exceed it and you stress the container structure, the stack it sits in, and the ship's stability calculation. Overweight containers are a documented cause of damage and handling accidents. This one's about more than money.

How to know before it's too late

The fix is checking before you pack, not after the port calls. Every container has three published weights — the tare, the payload and the gross — and your cargo has to stay under the payload. When you're estimating "how many cartons fit," the number that matters is whichever cuts off first: volume or weight.
